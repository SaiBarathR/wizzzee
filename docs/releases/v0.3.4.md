# Wizzzee 0.3.4

Wizzzee is a disk space analyzer for macOS in the shape of [WizTree](https://diskanalyzer.com/): a folder tree with sizes, a list of the biggest files, and a colored treemap you can click into. It scans a full startup disk — around 3.9 million files — in roughly 15 seconds.

**Upgrade if a scan has ever told you it found more than your disk can hold.** On a 995 GB disk the header read **Scanned: 1.7 TB**, and the home folder showed as 1.6 TB. Nothing had been counted twice and nothing on the disk was wrong: one file was being measured by its length instead of by the space it takes up. That is fixed here, everywhere it showed.

## A scan could add up to more than the disk

Some files are longer than the space they occupy. A virtual machine or container image is created at the size of the disk it pretends to be, and filled in as it is used; the parts nobody has written to yet take up nothing. These are called *sparse* files. On the Mac this was found on, OrbStack's image is 995 GB long and occupies 41.6 GB — on a disk of 995 GB.

Wizzzee has always kept both figures, and has a control at the top right for which one to show: **Size**, the length, or **On Disk**, the space taken. On Disk is the default. The treemap, the percentage bars and the sort order all followed that control. The lines that *sum up* the scan did not — they added up lengths whatever it said:

- the **Scanned** figure in the header
- the size the status bar gives for whatever is selected
- the running total under the Scan button while a scan is in progress
- the figure above the treemap as you hover over it

So the window drew a treemap of 772 GB under a header announcing 1.7 TB, right above the line giving the disk's capacity as 995 GB.

All of them now report whichever measure is on show. With On Disk showing, that same scan reads **Scanned: 772 GB**. Switch to Size and it gives the length *and* what that occupies — `1.7 TB • 772 GB on disk` — because a length is the one figure that can exceed the disk, and it should never sit next to the disk's capacity unexplained. The status bar's **Total**, which was that same sum of lengths, is now called what it is: **Logical size**.

## The tables put space on disk first

The Tree View and the File View both listed **Size** and then **Allocated**. The first number you read for a folder was therefore its files' combined length — 1.2 TB for the folder holding that image — sitting beside a percentage bar worked out from the 198 GB it actually occupies.

The columns are now **On Disk** and then **Size**, named as the control names them. Whichever one is not on show is dimmed.

Changing the control also re-sorts. If a table was ordered by the measure you were looking at, it is now ordered by the one you switched to; before, it stayed ranked by the old one under bars that no longer ran in order. An order you chose for its own sake — by name, by date, or by the measure that *wasn't* on show — is left alone.

## Sparse and cloud-only files are marked

Until now the only sign of a sparse file was two columns that disagreed, with nothing to say which to believe.

- A file with parts that were never written is marked **sparse**.
- A file whose contents are being held by iCloud or another cloud provider, and are not downloaded, is marked **online only**.

Hover over the mark for both figures. Compressed files also occupy less than their length — most of macOS's own programs do — but nothing of theirs is missing, so they are left unmarked.

The sparse mark appears on APFS volumes, which is what every modern Mac's own disk uses. On other kinds of volume the difference between the two columns is still shown but not explained, because there it can mean other things.

## A delete could promise space it wouldn't free

With **Size** showing, the confirmation before a permanent delete quoted the file's length: delete that image and it said 995 GB would be reclaimed. It frees 41.6 GB. The confirmation now states the space on disk whichever measure the tables are in.

## Small things

- The **Hard links** figure in the status bar was a sum of lengths, quoted beside a total of space on disk. It is now space on disk as well.
- The treemap's tooltip led with the length even when the tiles were sized by space on disk. It now leads with whichever the tiles are drawn from, and names both.
- `Wizzzee --scan` ranked its lists of the largest folders, files and types by length, so one sparse image headed all three. They are ranked by space on disk — the figure `du` prints — with the length in brackets where it differs.

## If you compare Wizzzee with another tool

This bug was found by checking a scan against CleanMyMac's Space Lens. With it fixed, the two still won't agree to the gigabyte, and neither is wrong:

- **Hard links.** A file can have more than one name. Wizzzee counts its space once, as `du` does. A tool that counts every name in full shows folders full of hard links — package stores, developer caches — as larger. On the Mac in question that was 23 GB of the home folder. The system's own `/bin` shows it in miniature: 6.3 MB in Wizzzee and `du`, 7.2 MB with each name counted.
- **Used space.** Wizzzee's **Volume Used** is everything not physically free. macOS also keeps *purgeable* space — caches and local snapshots it will delete when room is needed — and Finder and some tools count that as available. That is why Wizzzee can say 807 GB used where another says 763 GB.

## Installing

Download `Wizzzee-0.3.4-macos-universal.zip` below, unzip it, and move `Wizzzee.app` to Applications, replacing the old one.

- **First launch.** The app is not notarized, so macOS blocks it the first time. Open it once, dismiss the warning, then go to **System Settings → Privacy & Security** and click **Open Anyway**. macOS asks only once.
- **Full Disk Access has to be granted again.** macOS ties that permission to the exact build, so the grant from an earlier version does not carry over. In **System Settings → Privacy & Security → Full Disk Access**, remove the old Wizzzee entry and add the new app. Without it, Mail, Messages, Safari and other users' folders are skipped and the totals read low.

It runs on macOS 14 or newer, on Apple silicon and Intel.

## Internal

The scanner's numbers were never wrong. Space on disk matched `du -sk` to the kilobyte on every folder it was checked against, before this change and after. What was wrong was which of two correct numbers got shown, in the handful of places that had been written to read a file's length directly rather than ask which measure was on show. Those places now ask.

A sparse file is recognised from what the scan already reads rather than by asking the filesystem for its own flag, which would have cost every scan a second set of attributes per file. The two were compared over the 3.1 million files of a home folder and picked out the same 85. Scan time and peak memory are unchanged: 10.5–11.2 seconds and 457–460 MB on that folder, before and after.

The change was reviewed three times by a second model before it was merged. The first pass found seven problems in it — among them, that marking sparse files by that rule would have mislabelled every file on a network share that reports no allocation — and all were fixed before anything shipped.

The self-test grew from 249 checks to 297. One builds a sparse file twice the length of the volume it sits on and checks that what the scan reports still fits on that volume. Each new check was confirmed to fail with its fix taken out, and the suite runs clean under both the thread and the address sanitizer.

One thing was not done. 0.3.3 came out of a model clicking through the released app, and the same pass was planned here; it could not run, because the test Mac's screen was locked. The window was instead rendered headlessly in both measures and read by eye, and what the control and the tables do when clicked is covered by the self-test — but this build has not been clicked through.
